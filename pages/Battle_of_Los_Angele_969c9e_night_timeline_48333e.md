## Introduction

The night of 24–25 February 1942 became one of the most dramatic air-defence incidents in the United States during the Second World War. In the early hours over Los Angeles, a suspected aerial threat triggered a city-wide blackout, searchlight sweeps, and a major anti-aircraft barrage. Yet when the guns stopped, no enemy aircraft had been confirmed, no bombs had fallen, and no attacking force was found. The episode became known as the “[Battle of Los Angeles]({{ 'battle-of-los-angeles-1942/' | relative_url }})” because it captured the tension between a real wartime threat environment and the uncertainty of identifying what was actually happening in the sky.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.laalmanac.com/history/hi07s.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: laalmanac.com">[La Almanac]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">laalmanac.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">La AlmanacThe Mysterious Battle of Los Angeles, 1942...</span></span></span>

## Rising fears after Pearl Harbor and Ellwood

Los Angeles entered February 1942 with genuine fears of attack. The Japanese assault on Pearl Harbor two months earlier had changed the strategic situation on the American Pacific coast, and Southern California was considered a particularly sensitive target because of its aircraft factories, naval facilities, and defence industries. The possibility of enemy aircraft reaching the mainland was treated as a serious military concern rather than an abstract fear.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.laalmanac.com/history/hi07s.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: laalmanac.com">[La Almanac]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">laalmanac.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">La AlmanacThe Mysterious Battle of Los Angeles, 1942...</span></span></span>

The anxiety intensified after the Japanese submarine attack on the Ellwood oil facilities near Santa Barbara on 23 February 1942. The submarine I-17 surfaced offshore and shelled the coastline, causing limited damage but demonstrating that the war could directly reach the American mainland. For residents and military planners in California, the incident increased expectations that another attack might follow.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.laalmanac.com/history/hi07s.php" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: laalmanac.com">[La Almanac]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">laalmanac.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">La AlmanacThe Mysterious Battle of Los Angeles, 1942...</span></span></span>

On the evening of 24 February, coastal defences were already operating in a heightened state of alert. Reports of unusual lights and possible aircraft activity around defence areas added to the atmosphere of uncertainty. The earlier warning state was lifted later that evening, but concern returned after new reports during the early morning hours.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Los_Angeles" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Battle of Los Angeles</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Battle of Los Angeles</span></span></span>

The crucial problem facing commanders was not simply whether an attack was occurring, but whether they could afford to ignore a possible attack. The memory of Pearl Harbor encouraged a cautious approach: a false alarm would be embarrassing, but failing to respond to a real raid could have been disastrous.

## The blackout and anti-aircraft response

The main sequence of events began shortly after midnight and accelerated in the early hours of 25 February. Radar reports indicated an unidentified presence approaching the Los Angeles area, and military authorities prepared the city’s defences. At about 2:25 a.m., air-raid sirens sounded across Los Angeles County, a blackout was ordered, and air-raid wardens moved into action.
