## Introduction

The All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO) investigation into the USS Theodore Roosevelt “Go Fast” video concluded that the object shown in the 2015 Navy infrared footage did not demonstrate extraordinary speed or flight characteristics. AARO’s analysis found that the apparent rapid movement was primarily a result of viewing geometry, especially motion parallax, rather than evidence of a craft accelerating or travelling at extreme velocity. The office estimated the object was about 13,000 feet above sea level and moving in a manner consistent with ordinary atmospheric motion after accounting for wind.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/case_resolution_reports/AARO_GoFast_Case_Resolution_Card_Methodology_Final.pdf?ver=qXvjrruO_0MgtnTsfYnCEQ%3D%3D" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Go Fast Case Resolution Card Methodology Final</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNCLASSIFIEDMay 10, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 10, 2026</span></span></span>

The finding is significant because “Go Fast” became one of the most discussed examples of modern Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena (UAP) evidence: a genuine Navy recording involving military sensors and trained aviators, yet one where the strongest initial impression — an object racing just above the ocean — changed substantially after technical reconstruction. ## The government analysis process

AARO approached the Go Fast case as a sensor-analysis problem rather than relying only on the visual impression of the video. The footage was captured in January 2015 by a U.S. Navy F/A-18F Super Hornet using a forward-looking infrared (FLIR) targeting pod. The Department of Defense later released the clip publicly in 2020, identifying it as authentic Navy imagery while retaining the object’s classification as unidentified at that time. AARO’s later review examined the available video information, including the sensor display data embedded in the recording.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/UAP-Cases/Official-UAP-Imagery/4/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UAP Imagery</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AARO UAP Imagery...</span></span></span>

A central part of the investigation was reconstructing the relationship between the observing aircraft and the object. Infrared footage can show an object’s apparent movement across a screen, but that movement does not automatically reveal its real speed through the atmosphere. Analysts must consider factors such as the sensor’s viewing angle, target range, aircraft movement, altitude differences, and wind conditions. AARO’s methodology paper explained that it extracted information from the publicly available video because original sensor metadata was not available for the analysis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/case_resolution_reports/AARO_GoFast_Case_Resolution_Card_Methodology_Final.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Go Fast Case Resolution</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AARO GoFast Case Resolution...</span></span></span>

This approach reflects a broader issue in UAP investigations: a recording can be authentic while still being difficult to interpret. The question AARO attempted to answer was not whether the video was real, but whether the object’s observed behaviour required an unusual explanation. Its conclusion was that the available evidence did not support that interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/case_resolution_reports/AARO_GoFast_Case_Resolution_Card_Methodology_Final.pdf?ver=qXvjrruO_0MgtnTsfYnCEQ%3D%3D" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Go Fast Case Resolution Card Methodology Final</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNCLASSIFIEDMay 10, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 10, 2026</span></span></span>

## Why the object appeared to move so fast

The main explanation offered by AARO was motion parallax. This occurs when an observer moves relative to a distant object, making that object appear to move rapidly against a closer background even when the object itself is moving slowly.

In the Go Fast video, the object appears to skim across the ocean surface because the camera is looking downward from a fast-moving aircraft. The ocean provides a strong visual reference, but the object was not actually close to the water. AARO calculated the object’s altitude at approximately 3,962 metres (13,000 feet) and found that the apparent speed was amplified by the changing viewing geometry between the aircraft, the object, and the background.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/case_resolution_reports/AARO_GoFast_Case_Resolution_Card_Methodology_Final.pdf?ver=qXvjrruO_0MgtnTsfYnCEQ%3D%3D" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Go Fast Case Resolution Card Methodology Final</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNCLASSIFIEDMay 10, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 10, 2026</span></span></span>

The effect can be understood through a simple comparison: a person looking out of a moving car may see nearby objects rush past while distant mountains barely appear to move. The same principle applies in the air, where a sensor mounted on a fast-moving aircraft can create a misleading impression of speed when tracking a distant target.

AARO’s analysis modelled different possible flight paths and wind conditions. The report concluded that the object’s apparent motion could be explained without requiring unusual propulsion or manoeuvring. Under some assumptions, the object’s movement was close to the surrounding wind direction, while other assumptions produced somewhat higher speeds. AARO emphasised that these values depended on uncertainties in aircraft heading and atmospheric conditions, but none of the tested scenarios indicated anomalous performance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/case_resolution_reports/AARO_GoFast_Case_Resolution_Card_Methodology_Final.pdf?ver=qXvjrruO_0MgtnTsfYnCEQ%3D%3D" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Go Fast Case Resolution Card Methodology Final</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNCLASSIFIEDMay 10, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 10, 2026</span></span></span>

## What the sensor and weather data showed

The most important evidence in AARO’s review came from combining the FLIR display information with atmospheric data. The office used the target’s apparent position, sensor angles, range information, and altitude estimates to reconstruct its location. It then compared possible movement patterns against historical wind conditions at relevant altitudes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/case_resolution_reports/AARO_GoFast_Case_Resolution_Card_Methodology_Final.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Go Fast Case Resolution</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AARO GoFast Case Resolution...</span></span></span>

AARO assessed that the object was operating at roughly the altitude of a small airborne object rather than immediately above the ocean. The analysis found that its movement was compatible with wind-influenced travel rather than a vehicle actively demonstrating exceptional speed or control.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/case_resolution_reports/AARO_GoFast_Case_Resolution_Card_Methodology_Final.pdf?ver=qXvjrruO_0MgtnTsfYnCEQ%3D%3D" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Go Fast Case Resolution Card Methodology Final</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNCLASSIFIEDMay 10, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 10, 2026</span></span></span>

NASA’s independent UAP study also highlighted the Go Fast video as an example of how numerical information from a sensor display can change the interpretation of an event. ## What remains unidentified

AARO’s conclusion resolved the central anomaly claimed by the video: the object was not shown to be travelling at impossible or highly unusual speeds. However, the office did not assign a definitive identity to the object itself. The object remains a case where the available evidence was sufficient to explain the apparent behaviour but insufficient to determine exactly what physical object produced the infrared signature.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/UAP-Cases/Official-UAP-Imagery/4/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UAP Imagery</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AARO UAP Imagery...</span></span></span>

This distinction is important in UAP investigations. “Unidentified” does not necessarily mean “unknown technology”; it means that the available information does not allow a confident identification. AARO’s review separated two questions that are often combined in public discussion:

* **Did the video show something unusual?** The apparent motion was unusual and understandably attracted attention.
* **Did the data demonstrate extraordinary capability?** AARO concluded that it did not.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/case_resolution_reports/AARO_GoFast_Case_Resolution_Card_Methodology_Final.pdf?ver=qXvjrruO_0MgtnTsfYnCEQ%3D%3D" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Go Fast Case Resolution Card Methodology Final</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNCLASSIFIEDMay 10, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 10, 2026</span></span></span>

The Go Fast case therefore became an example of how official investigations can narrow a mystery without eliminating every uncertainty.
