## Introduction

Hypnosis changed the Larson case from a report of unusual lights and missing time into one of the most detailed alleged abduction narratives of the 1970s. Before hypnotic sessions, Sandra “Sandy” Larson, her daughter Jackie, and Jackie’s boyfriend Terry O’Leary were associated mainly with a reported UFO sighting near Buffalo, North Dakota, on 26 August 1975. The later regression sessions conducted with psychologist R. Leo Sprinkle produced far more elaborate memories: an apparent encounter inside a craft, medical procedures, and encounters with unusual beings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cufos.org/PDFs/HUMCAT/HUMCAT_Index_1975.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufos.org">[Center for UFO Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">HUMCAT Index 1975</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Center for UFO Studies1975 AddendaNovember 1, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 1, 2025</span></span></span>

The importance of the hypnosis element is not that it proves or disproves the alleged abduction. Rather, it shows how a case can move from an ambiguous event into a structured narrative through the interaction of memory, interpretation, and investigative technique. Supporters of the case viewed hypnosis as a way to recover details hidden by trauma or missing time. Critics argued that hypnotic recall can increase confidence in memories without guaranteeing their accuracy, especially when the subject matter is already surrounded by expectations about UFO encounters.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://novel-coronavirus.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/pdf/10.1002/0470846402.ch7" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: novel-coronavirus.onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">novel-coronavirus.onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Online Library Hypnosis and Recovered Memory: Evidence‐Based Practice</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryHypnosis and Recovered Memory: Evidence‐Based Practice - McConkey - 2001 - Wiley Online Books - Wiley Online LibraryA...</span></span></span>

## Sandra Larson’s recalled experience

The original 1975 event contained an unexplained gap: the witnesses reported seeing unusual glowing objects and later believed that a period of time had passed without a clear memory of what occurred. That uncertainty became the opening through which the abduction interpretation developed. The later hypnotic account attributed the missing period to an encounter with occupants of a UFO rather than simply an unknown interruption during the drive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cufos.org/PDFs/HUMCAT/HUMCAT_Index_1975.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufos.org">[Center for UFO Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">HUMCAT Index 1975</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Center for UFO Studies1975 AddendaNovember 1, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 1, 2025</span></span></span>

According to case summaries preserved in UFO research archives, Sandra Larson’s hypnotic memories included being removed from the vehicle and taken aboard a craft. She described a humanoid figure with unusual physical characteristics and recalled a medical-style examination. These details became among the most frequently repeated elements of the Larson case because they transformed a sighting report into a classic “close encounter” narrative.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cufos.org/PDFs/HUMCAT/HUMCAT_Index_1975.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufos.org">[Center for UFO Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">HUMCAT Index 1975</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Center for UFO Studies1975 AddendaNovember 1, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 1, 2025</span></span></span>

The role of R. Leo Sprinkle was significant because he was one of the earliest psychologists to investigate alleged UFO abduction experiences using hypnosis. He began studying UFO-related reports at the University of Wyoming in the 1960s and later conducted hypnotic sessions with people who reported such experiences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/ufosymposium/sprinkle.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[NCAS Files]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NCAS FilesUFO Symposium 1968: Sprinkle Statement...</span></span></span> His involvement gave the Larson case a degree of psychological framing that distinguished it from ordinary UFO reports, but it also made the case part of a wider debate over whether hypnosis was an investigative tool or a source of narrative construction.

## Jackie’s different memories

One of the most important features of the Larson case is that the hypnotic accounts were not identical. Sandra’s recalled experience focused heavily on being taken inside a craft and undergoing examination, while Jackie Larson’s memories reportedly involved different sensations and events, including paralysis and separation from the vehicle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cufos.org/PDFs/HUMCAT/HUMCAT_Index_1975.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufos.org">[Center for UFO Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">HUMCAT Index 1975</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Center for UFO Studies1975 AddendaNovember 1, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 1, 2025</span></span></span>

For some UFO researchers, differences between witnesses were not necessarily a weakness. They argued that people experiencing a confusing or traumatic event might remember different parts of the same incident. Separate memories could therefore be seen as complementary rather than contradictory.

Sceptics interpret the same feature differently. If multiple witnesses were exposed to the same event but later produced different detailed narratives under hypnosis, critics argue that this raises questions about how much of the account came from the original experience and how much came from later interpretation. Memory research has repeatedly shown that recollection is not a simple recording of events; it can be influenced by expectations, questions, later information, and the context in which recall occurs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://staff.washington.edu/eloftus/Articles/hoff.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: staff.washington.edu">[UW Staff]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">staff.washington.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UW StaffMisinformation and Memory The Creation of New Memories (1989)March 1, 1989...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 1, 1989</span></span></span>

The Larson case therefore highlights a central difficulty in evaluating hypnotically recalled testimony: a person can be sincere and confident while still having memories that have changed over time.

## Why hypnosis became controversial in UFO cases

### The promise of recovering missing memories

During the 1970s, hypnotic regression became increasingly associated with UFO abduction investigations. Researchers interested in abduction reports often viewed hypnosis as a way to access memories that were unavailable during ordinary questioning. The method seemed particularly attractive in cases involving “missing time”, where witnesses believed something significant had happened but could not initially describe it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alien_abduction" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Alien abduction</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Alien abduction</span></span></span>

In the Larson case, hypnosis appeared to provide the missing explanation: the lost period was no longer an unexplained interval but a detailed sequence of events. This was the mechanism that gave the case its enduring place in abduction literature.

However, memory specialists have long warned that hypnosis is not equivalent to replaying a recording. Studies and reviews of recovered memories have found that memory can be accurate in some circumstances but is also vulnerable to suggestion and distortion. Hypnosis may increase the amount of information a person reports, but it does not reliably separate true memories from imagined or constructed details.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1500609/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Reports of real and false memories: the relevance of hypnosis, hypnotizability, and context of memory test - PubMed...</span></span></span>

### The problem of confidence versus accuracy

A major issue in the Larson case is the difference between how convincing a memory feels and whether it can be independently verified. Hypnotic sessions can produce vivid imagery and strong emotional conviction, but vividness alone is not evidence that every recalled detail occurred exactly as remembered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev.clinpsy.2.022305.095315" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: annualreviews.org">[Annual Reviews]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">annualreviews.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Annual Reviews Recovered Memories &#124; Annual Reviews</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Annual Reviews Recovered Memories &#124; Annual Reviews</span></span></span>

This distinction matters because many of the most dramatic elements of the Larson account come from hypnotic recall rather than from independent evidence. The initial sighting involved multiple witnesses, but the detailed interior-craft narrative depended primarily on memories recovered later.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cufos.org/PDFs/HUMCAT/HUMCAT_Index_1975.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufos.org">[Center for UFO Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">HUMCAT Index 1975</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Center for UFO Studies1975 AddendaNovember 1, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 1, 2025</span></span></span>

For supporters, this does not eliminate the possibility that the memories reflect a real but unusual experience. For critics, the lack of external confirmation means the hypnotic material must be treated cautiously.

## Debates over recovered memory reliability

The Larson case became part of a broader dispute that extended beyond UFO research: whether hypnosis can uncover forgotten events or unintentionally create convincing but inaccurate memories.

Research by psychologists studying false memories has shown that people can incorporate suggested information into their recollections and later remember those additions as if they were part of the original event. Elizabeth Loftus and other researchers demonstrated that memory can be altered by misleading information introduced after an event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://staff.washington.edu/eloftus/Articles/hoff.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: staff.washington.edu">[UW Staff]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">staff.washington.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UW StaffMisinformation and Memory The Creation of New Memories (1989)March 1, 1989...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 1, 1989</span></span></span> Reviews of recovered-memory controversies have similarly emphasised that memory retrieval is reconstructive and that certain techniques can increase the risk of false or distorted recollections.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev.clinpsy.2.022305.095315" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: annualreviews.org">[Annual Reviews]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">annualreviews.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Annual Reviews Recovered Memories &#124; Annual Reviews</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Annual Reviews Recovered Memories &#124; Annual Reviews</span></span></span>

Applied to the Larson case, this does not mean the witnesses deliberately invented their accounts. The key issue is whether the hypnotic process revealed previously inaccessible information or helped organise uncertainty into a culturally familiar abduction story.

That question became especially important because UFO abduction narratives of the period often shared recurring features: missing time, examinations, unusual beings, and encounters inside a craft. Researchers who accepted the phenomenon saw these similarities as evidence of a genuine underlying pattern. Sceptics argued that similarities could also result from shared expectations, popular culture, and the influence of investigators who were already familiar with earlier abduction reports.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narrative_of_the_abduction_phenomenon" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Narrative of the abduction phenomenon</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Narrative of the abduction phenomenon</span></span></span>

## What hypnosis changed about the Larson case

The lasting significance of the Larson case lies in the transition between two different kinds of evidence. The first was an external event: three people reporting unusual lights and a strange experience on a road near Fargo. The second was an internally recovered narrative: detailed memories of an abduction that emerged through hypnosis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cufos.org/PDFs/HUMCAT/HUMCAT_Index_1975.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufos.org">[Center for UFO Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">HUMCAT Index 1975</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Center for UFO Studies1975 AddendaNovember 1, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 1, 2025</span></span></span>

That transition explains why the case remains debated. The hypnotic sessions did not simply add; they changed the category of the event. A puzzling sighting became a fully developed abduction account, bringing with it all the larger questions surrounding recovered memories, witness testimony, and the reliability of regression techniques.

The Larson story is therefore less a simple question of whether hypnosis “revealed the truth” or “created a false memory”. Its importance comes from showing how uncertain experiences can become powerful narratives when memory recovery, interpretation, and belief systems interact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://novel-coronavirus.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/pdf/10.1002/0470846402.ch7" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: novel-coronavirus.onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">novel-coronavirus.onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Online Library Hypnosis and Recovered Memory: Evidence‐Based Practice</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryHypnosis and Recovered Memory: Evidence‐Based Practice - McConkey - 2001 - Wiley Online Books - Wiley Online LibraryA...</span></span></span>
