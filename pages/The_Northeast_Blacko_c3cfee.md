## Introduction

The blackout became a landmark event in electrical engineering because it exposed the risks of increasingly interconnected power grids. The failure was not simply that one component malfunctioned; the deeper problem was that a small disturbance could spread through a heavily loaded system without sufficient monitoring and safeguards. ## How the blackout unfolded on 9 November 1965

The blackout began during the evening rush period, when electricity demand was high because of cold weather and widespread use of heating, lighting and household appliances. At the centre of the failure was the Sir Adam Beck generating station near Niagara Falls, Ontario, part of the Ontario Hydro system. A protective relay — an automatic safety device designed to disconnect equipment under abnormal electrical conditions — had been set incorrectly on one of the transmission lines leaving the station.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northeast_blackout_of_1965" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Northeast blackout of 1965</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Northeast blackout of 1965</span></span></span>

At approximately 5:16 p.m. Eastern Time, a small fluctuation in the power flow caused the relay to operate earlier than intended. The transmission line disconnected, forcing electricity onto neighbouring lines. Those lines then became overloaded and their protective systems also activated, producing a chain reaction. Within minutes, generating stations and transmission links across the region separated themselves to prevent further damage, but the result was a widespread loss of supply.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theblackoutreport.co.uk/2019/11/11/northeastern-blackout-usa-canada-1965/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theblackoutreport.co.uk">[the Blackout report]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theblackoutreport.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">northeastern blackout usa canada 1965</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the Blackout reportThe Northeastern Blackout 1965 &#124; the Blackout reportNovember 11, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 11, 2019</span></span></span>

The interconnected nature of the grid was both the reason electricity could normally be shared efficiently across large distances and the reason a local problem could become a regional crisis. The Federal Power Commission report documented the sequence of line openings and the difficulties involved in restoring service across the affected network.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://books.google.com/books/about/Northeast_Power_Failure.html?id=dUNSAQAAIAAJ" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: books.google.com">[Google Books]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">books.google.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Northeast Power Failure</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Google BooksNortheast Power Failure: November 9 and 10, 1965: a Report to the President - United States. ## What people experienced during the 14-hour outage

The blackout affected an area of roughly 80,000 square miles, leaving millions without electricity for periods ranging from several hours to around 13 or 14 hours depending on location. New York City was among the most visible examples of the disruption. Office buildings went dark, traffic signals failed, subway trains stopped, and many passengers became stranded in stations, tunnels and elevators.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.baruch.cuny.edu/nycdata/disasters/blackouts-northeast.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: baruch.cuny.edu">[Baruch College]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">baruch.cuny.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Baruch College -NYCdata &#124; Disasters</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Baruch College -NYCdata &#124; Disasters</span></span></span>

Despite the scale of the disruption, the social response was notably calm compared with later urban blackouts. Emergency workers helped rescue people trapped in lifts and transport systems, while residents used candles, flashlights and community support networks to cope with the darkness. Contemporary accounts highlighted that the event created inconvenience and confusion but did not produce widespread disorder.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.baruch.cuny.edu/nycdata/disasters/blackouts-northeast.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: baruch.cuny.edu">[Baruch College]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">baruch.cuny.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Baruch College -NYCdata &#124; Disasters</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Baruch College -NYCdata &#124; Disasters</span></span></span>

The blackout also demonstrated how dependent modern cities had become on continuous electricity. Communication systems, transport infrastructure, businesses and public services were all affected, reinforcing concerns about the reliability of increasingly complex electrical networks. President Lyndon B. Johnson described the event as a clear demonstration of society’s dependence on an uninterrupted power supply when responding to the Federal Power Commission investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/statement-the-president-response-federal-power-commission-report-the-northeastern-electric" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: presidency.ucsb.edu">[The American Presidency Project]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">presidency.ucsb.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The American Presidency ProjectStatement by the President in Response to Federal Power Commission Report on the Northeastern Electric Pow...</span></span></span>

## The official investigation and the confirmed cause

The Federal Power Commission, working with American and Canadian utility experts, investigated both the initial trigger and the reason the failure spread so widely. Its December 1965 report concluded that the immediate cause was the operation of a protective relay that had been improperly set, while broader contributing factors included the design and coordination of the interconnected transmission system.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://books.google.com/books/about/Northeast_Power_Failure.html?id=dUNSAQAAIAAJ" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: books.google.com">[Google Books]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">books.google.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Northeast Power Failure</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Google BooksNortheast Power Failure: November 9 and 10, 1965: a Report to the President - United States. The investigation shifted attention away from the idea that a blackout of such size required a dramatic single failure. Instead, it showed how ordinary engineering decisions could create unexpected vulnerabilities when combined with high demand and limited system awareness. A relay had performed its intended safety function, but because it was configured incorrectly and the wider network lacked enough protection against cascading effects, the safety mechanism became the starting point of a much larger collapse.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theblackoutreport.co.uk/2019/11/11/northeastern-blackout-usa-canada-1965/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theblackoutreport.co.uk">[the Blackout report]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theblackoutreport.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">northeastern blackout usa canada 1965</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the Blackout reportThe Northeastern Blackout 1965 &#124; the Blackout reportNovember 11, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 11, 2019</span></span></span>

The recommendations that followed included improved coordination between utilities, stronger system monitoring, better planning for interconnected grids and more detailed studies of how disturbances could travel through transmission networks. ## Competing explanations and common misconceptions

Because the blackout occurred during a period of Cold War tension and growing public interest in unidentified flying objects, some rumours and speculative explanations circulated at the time. Stories suggesting unusual causes, including claims involving UFOs or mysterious external forces, were not supported by the technical evidence gathered during the official investigation. The documented chain of events pointed to an identifiable engineering failure involving grid protection equipment and transmission overloads.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://books.google.com/books/about/Northeast_Power_Failure.html?id=dUNSAQAAIAAJ" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: books.google.com">[Google Books]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">books.google.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Northeast Power Failure</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Google BooksNortheast Power Failure: November 9 and 10, 1965: a Report to the President - United States. Federal Power Commission - Goog...</span></span></span>

The strongest evidence comes from the physical operation of the power system itself: recorded electrical behaviour, relay actions, equipment responses and the reconstruction of the sequence of failures by engineers. Unlike cases where evidence depends mainly on witness testimony, the blackout left extensive technical records that allowed investigators to trace the event step by step.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://books.google.com/books/about/Northeast_Power_Failure.html?id=dUNSAQAAIAAJ" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: books.google.com">[Google Books]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">books.google.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Northeast Power Failure</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Google BooksNortheast Power Failure: November 9 and 10, 1965: a Report to the President - United States. The unresolved questions were therefore not about whether an unknown force caused the blackout, but about why the electrical system allowed a relatively small disturbance to expand into a regional failure. Engineers focused on system design, communication between utilities and the need for better safeguards against cascading failures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://books.google.com/books/about/Northeast_Power_Failure.html?id=dUNSAQAAIAAJ" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: books.google.com">[Google Books]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">books.google.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Northeast Power Failure</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Google BooksNortheast Power Failure: November 9 and 10, 1965: a Report to the President - United States. ## Why the 1965 blackout changed power-grid thinking

The Northeast Blackout of 1965 became a defining lesson in the risks of large interconnected electricity networks. Before the event, many planners assumed that automatic protections and the ability to transfer power between utilities would make the system highly reliable. The blackout showed that interconnection could also amplify failures when a disturbance moved faster than operators could respond.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://books.google.com/books/about/Northeast_Power_Failure.html?id=dUNSAQAAIAAJ" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: books.google.com">[Google Books]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">books.google.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Northeast Power Failure</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Google BooksNortheast Power Failure: November 9 and 10, 1965: a Report to the President - United States.
