## Introduction

The United States Air Force investigation of the Rex Heflin photographs became one of the most disputed episodes in Project Blue Book’s history because the official explanation did not rest on a simple identification of the object. Instead, Air Force investigators moved from examining the photographs as possible evidence of an unknown aerial object to treating them as a likely photographic hoax. The case was therefore important not only because of what the images appeared to show, but because it exposed disagreements inside and outside the investigation over how photographic evidence should be judged.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/case52.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[files.ncas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Condon Report, Case 52: Traffic Investigator Photographs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">August 3, 1965...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 3, 1965</span></span></span>

Project Blue Book’s final position was that the photographs did not provide reliable evidence of an extraordinary aircraft. Critics of that conclusion argued that the Air Force’s hoax assessment relied too heavily on a simplified reproduction experiment and did not fully resolve questions about Heflin’s credibility, the missing original photographs, or the unusual features reported in the image sequence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/case52.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[files.ncas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Condon Report, Case 52: Traffic Investigator Photographs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">August 3, 1965...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 3, 1965</span></span></span> The result was a case that remained officially explained but publicly contested.

## Project Blue Book examination

### How the Air Force approached the case

Project Blue Book investigated the Heflin photographs as part of its wider mission of evaluating reported UFO sightings for the Air Force. The programme, active from 1952 to 1969, collected witness reports, photographs, and technical assessments before reaching classifications ranging from identified conventional objects to unexplained cases. Its records were later transferred to the National Archives, where the files remain available for research.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National Archives...</span></span></span>

After Heflin reported photographing a disc-shaped object near Santa Ana, California, Air Force investigators reviewed the available photographic material and interviewed him. A significant difficulty was that the original Polaroid photographs were not available to investigators at the time of the main analysis. The images had reportedly been taken from Heflin’s possession by people claiming a connection with NORAD and were unavailable for much of the early investigation, leaving analysts dependent on copies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://searchufos.com/cases/rex-heflin-ufo-photos.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: searchufos.com">[SearchUFOs]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">searchufos.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Search UFOs Rex Heflin UFO Photos — UFO Case File</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Search UFOs Rex Heflin UFO Photos — UFO Case File</span></span></span>

The case was examined by Project Blue Book personnel including Major Hector Quintanilla, who publicly stated in October 1965 that the Air Force had classified the images as a photographic hoax after analysis. The statement triggered immediate disagreement from UFO researchers, who argued that the conclusion went beyond what the photographic evidence could demonstrate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/case52.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[files.ncas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Condon Report, Case 52: Traffic Investigator Photographs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">August 3, 1965...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 3, 1965</span></span></span>

### The role of photographic analysis

The central question for Blue Book was not simply whether Heflin had seen something unusual, but whether the photographs themselves demonstrated the presence of an unknown object. Investigators focused on whether the apparent disc could have been produced by a small model or other artificial object photographed under controlled conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/case52.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[files.ncas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Condon Report, Case 52: Traffic Investigator Photographs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">August 3, 1965...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 3, 1965</span></span></span>

The later Condon Committee investigation, conducted for the University of Colorado’s scientific study of UFOs, also reviewed the Heflin case. Investigator William K. Hartmann examined the photographs and attempted to reproduce their appearance using a suspended model. His conclusion was that the evidence was insufficient to establish an extraordinary phenomenon. The Condon analysis described the case as having “inconclusive” evidence and highlighted problems such as uncertainty over the object’s distance, size, and the interpretation of the fourth photograph showing the reported smoke ring.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/case52.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[files.ncas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Condon Report, Case 52: Traffic Investigator Photographs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">August 3, 1965...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 3, 1965</span></span></span>

For official investigators, the inability to obtain firm physical measurements from the photographs was a major weakness. Without independent information about scale, distance, or motion, a photograph alone could not establish whether the object was a large airborne craft or a smaller object closer to the camera.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/case52.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[files.ncas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Condon Report, Case 52: Traffic Investigator Photographs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">August 3, 1965...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 3, 1965</span></span></span>

## The hoax interpretation and its critics

### Why the Air Force considered a hoax possible

The Air Force’s hoax interpretation rested mainly on the idea that the photographs could be recreated using a small object suspended in the correct position relative to the camera. Hartmann’s later Condon analysis similarly argued that a model could reproduce important visual characteristics of the images. This approach did not claim to identify the exact object used; rather, it argued that a conventional explanation was sufficient because the photographs did not require an extraordinary one.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/case52.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[files.ncas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Condon Report, Case 52: Traffic Investigator Photographs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">August 3, 1965...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 3, 1965</span></span></span>

This distinction became central to the dispute. Supporters of the official assessment argued that photographic evidence must meet a high standard before being accepted as proof of an unknown technology. A photograph that could plausibly be produced by ordinary means was considered inadequate evidence for an extraordinary claim.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/s3chap02.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[files.ncas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Condon Report Section III, Chapter 2: Photographic Evidence</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Condon Report Section III, Chapter 2: Photographic Evidence</span></span></span>

### Why the conclusion remained controversial


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

Critics challenged the strength of the hoax explanation on several grounds. One argument was that reproducing the general appearance of a disc-shaped object did not necessarily prove that Heflin had created the photographs in that way. The critics also pointed to Heflin’s behaviour during the investigation, arguing that investigators who interviewed him considered him a credible witness even though the final classification went against his account.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://unidentifiedphenomena.com/incidents/the-heflin-ufo-incident-1965/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unidentifiedphenomena.com">[Unidentified Phenomena]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unidentifiedphenomena.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the heflin ufo incident 1965</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Unidentified PhenomenaThe Heflin UFO Incident (1965) – Unidentified Phenomena...</span></span></span>

Because photographic examination is strongest when performed on original materials, analysts working from copies faced limitations in evaluating details of the film and image structure. The later return of the originals after many years allowed renewed interest in the case, but it could not fully undo the uncertainty created during the original investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://searchufos.com/cases/rex-heflin-ufo-photos.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: searchufos.com">[SearchUFOs]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">searchufos.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Search UFOs Rex Heflin UFO Photos — UFO Case File</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Search UFOs Rex Heflin UFO Photos — UFO Case File</span></span></span>

## Official investigations after 1965

### The Condon Committee reassessment

The Heflin photographs were later reviewed during the University of Colorado UFO study, commonly known as the Condon Committee investigation. Hartmann’s analysis became one of the most influential later assessments because it examined the photographs using a more formal scientific framework. The report concluded that the images did not provide sufficient evidence for an extraordinary object and noted weaknesses involving photographic interpretation and missing information about the scene.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/case52.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[files.ncas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Condon Report, Case 52: Traffic Investigator Photographs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">August 3, 1965...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 3, 1965</span></span></span>

The Condon analysis did not simply repeat the Air Force’s public statement. Instead, it placed the photographs within a broader scientific question: whether they contained measurable evidence that could not be explained by ordinary objects or photographic effects. Its conclusion was that they did not reach that threshold.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/case52.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[files.ncas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Condon Report, Case 52: Traffic Investigator Photographs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">August 3, 1965...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 3, 1965</span></span></span>

### The lasting status of the Air Force finding

Project Blue Book ended in 1969 after the Air Force concluded that its investigations had not found evidence that UFO reports represented threats to national security, technological developments beyond known science, or extraterrestrial vehicles. The agency’s overall conclusions applied to the programme as a whole rather than proving every individual case had a complete explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National Archives...</span></span></span>

For the Heflin photographs specifically, the lasting official position was that the images did not demonstrate an extraordinary aircraft and were best explained as a hoax or conventional photographic event. However, the case remained disputed because the evidence involved a credible witness, missing original photographs during key stages of investigation, and competing interpretations of whether the Air Force’s reproduction methods adequately matched the original circumstances.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/case52.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[files.ncas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Condon Report, Case 52: Traffic Investigator Photographs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">August 3, 1965...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 3, 1965</span></span></span>

The importance of the Project Blue Book investigation lies less in producing a universally accepted answer than in showing the limits of photographic evidence in UFO investigations. The Heflin case became an example of how official agencies, independent researchers, and later analysts could examine the same material yet reach different conclusions about what the evidence actually proved.
