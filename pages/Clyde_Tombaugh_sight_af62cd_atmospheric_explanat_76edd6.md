## Introduction

The atmospheric explanation for Clyde Tombaugh’s 1949 lights does not depend on proving that the sighting was a mistake. Instead, it asks whether a rare interaction between light and the atmosphere could have produced the brief appearance of a structured formation. Tombaugh himself, despite being the astronomer who discovered Pluto, favoured this type of explanation over an extraterrestrial craft, suggesting that a temperature inversion or other optical boundary in the atmosphere could have reflected or redirected light in an unusual way.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clyde_Tombaugh" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Clyde Tombaugh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Clyde Tombaugh</span></span></span>

The key idea is that the lights may not have been physical objects travelling through the sky. They could have been an optical image created when light passed through layers of air with different temperatures and densities. Such conditions can bend, distort, duplicate, compress, or displace images, sometimes creating effects that appear highly organised to an observer.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/physics-and-astronomy/atmospheric-optics" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Atmospheric Optics</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Atmospheric Optics - an overview &#124; ScienceDirect Topics...</span></span></span>
For Tombaugh’s sighting, this remains an explanation rather than a demonstrated solution: the observation lasted only seconds, and no meteorological measurements were recorded at the exact time and location.

## Optical effects in the atmosphere
## How a temperature inversion could create unusual lights

A temperature inversion occurs when warmer air lies above cooler air, reversing the normal pattern in which air generally becomes colder with altitude. These layers can create sharp changes in air density and therefore in the way light travels. Under suitable conditions, light rays can bend along curved paths rather than moving in a straight line, producing mirages and other displaced images.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://weather.metoffice.gov.uk/learn-about/weather/types-of-weather/temperature/temperature-inversion" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: weather.metoffice.gov.uk">[Met Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">weather.metoffice.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Met Office What is a temperature inversion?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Met OfficeWhat is a temperature inversion? - Met Office...</span></span></span>

This mechanism is important for Tombaugh’s report because he described the lights as a formation of faint bluish-green rectangles that changed appearance as they moved away from the overhead position. According to accounts of his observation, the formation became narrower, the individual shapes appeared foreshortened, and the brightness faded as it moved toward the southern horizon. Tombaugh later argued that this behaviour resembled the kind of distortion expected from an atmospheric optical boundary rather than from a solid object.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clyde_Tombaugh" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Clyde Tombaugh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Clyde Tombaugh</span></span></span>

An atmospheric layer can act somewhat like a weak lens or mirror. It does not create light by itself, but it can redirect existing light sources, including distant artificial lights, reflections, or other luminous sources, into unexpected positions. The resulting image may appear detached from its true source, making it difficult for an observer to identify what produced it.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/physics-and-astronomy/atmospheric-optics" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Atmospheric Optics</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Atmospheric Optics - an overview &#124; ScienceDirect Topics...</span></span></span>

## Why the shape of the lights matters


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

The unusual feature of the Tombaugh sighting was not simply that lights were seen, but that they appeared arranged in a geometric pattern. Atmospheric explanations therefore need to account for the apparent rectangular formation, not merely the colour or brightness.

</div>

Optical mirages are capable of producing distorted and repeated images rather than simple glowing patches. The World Meteorological Organization notes that mirages can produce images that are displaced, enlarged, reduced, inverted, or multiple, depending on atmospheric conditions.
## International Cloud Atlas
In principle, a structured source of light could retain some of its arrangement while being altered by refraction, creating an appearance that seems like a formation of separate objects.

The difficulty is that the exact source of the light in Tombaugh’s case is unknown. A complete atmospheric explanation would need to identify what light was being refracted and how the optical conditions produced the observed colour, spacing, and movement. Without photographs, instruments, or atmospheric records from the moment of the sighting, that reconstruction cannot be confirmed.

Tombaugh’s preferred explanation Why the witness himself favoured atmospheric optics<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/physics-and-astronomy/atmospheric-optics" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[sciencedirect.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect Atmospheric Optics</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Atmospheric Optics - an overview &#124; ScienceDirect Topics...</span></span></span>

Tombaugh’s later comments are significant because they show that the leading atmospheric interpretation did not originate only from outside sceptics. He did not regard the event as proof of a spacecraft. Instead, he considered a natural optical phenomenon more plausible, specifically mentioning the possibility of an atmospheric inversion or reflective boundary.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clyde_Tombaugh" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Clyde Tombaugh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Clyde Tombaugh</span></span></span>

His reasoning came from the behaviour of the phenomenon. Tombaugh noted that the lights were faint, appeared briefly, and faded in a way he associated with a changing viewing angle through an optical layer. He considered the rarity of such conditions a better explanation than an extraordinary physical visitor.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clyde_Tombaugh" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Clyde Tombaugh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Clyde Tombaugh</span></span></span>

This interpretation also reflects the distinction between astronomical expertise and atmospheric observation. Tombaugh was highly skilled at recognising celestial objects, but the event he described was not a conventional astronomical sighting. It involved light passing through the atmosphere, an area where unusual combinations of temperature, density, and viewing geometry can create effects that are unfamiliar even to experienced sky watchers.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/s6chap04.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[NCAS Files]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Files Condon Report, Sec VI, Chapter 4: Optical Mirage</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NCAS FilesCondon Report, Sec VI, Chapter 4: Optical Mirage...</span></span></span>

## The connection with other unusual light formations

The resemblance between Tombaugh’s report and other mid-century light formations contributed to later discussion of atmospheric explanations. Tombaugh himself compared the appearance to the later Lubbock Lights, another case involving bluish-green lights in formation.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://thinkaboutitdocs.com/astronomer-clyde-tombaugh-ufo-sighting-astronomer-who-discovered-pluto/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thinkaboutitdocs.com">[Think About It Docs]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thinkaboutitdocs.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">astronomer clyde tombaugh ufo sighting astronomer who discovered pluto</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Think About It Docs1949: The Clyde Tombaugh Sighting — Las Cruces, New MexicoApril 8, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 8, 2013</span></span></span>

However, similarity does not establish a common cause. A formation of lights can arise from several possibilities, including optical effects, reflections, or unidentified physical sources. The value of the atmospheric hypothesis is that it offers a mechanism for producing a structured appearance without requiring the lights themselves to be a structured craft.

## Why the cause remains uncertain

The strongest argument for an atmospheric explanation is that the sighting contains features that optical phenomena can potentially reproduce: rapid appearance and disappearance, changes in shape, fading with viewing angle, and the involvement of light rather than a clearly visible solid object. Atmospheric optics is a well-established field, and temperature gradients are known to create dramatic distortions of distant images.

The main limitation is the lack of direct evidence. No photograph, radar record, physical trace, or weather data from the exact moment of the observation can test the proposed mechanism. The explanation remains a reconstruction based on the visual description rather than a verified identification.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clyde_Tombaugh" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Clyde Tombaugh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Clyde Tombaugh</span></span></span>

The case therefore illustrates a broader challenge in analysing rare aerial observations. A credible witness can provide a careful description, yet the final interpretation may remain uncertain when the event is brief and unrepeatable. In Tombaugh’s case, atmospheric optics offers a scientifically plausible pathway for explaining how a strange formation of lights could appear, but the available evidence does not allow the precise optical process to be proven.
