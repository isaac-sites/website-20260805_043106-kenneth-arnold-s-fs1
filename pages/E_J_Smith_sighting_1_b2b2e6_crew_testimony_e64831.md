## Introduction

The Flight 105 sighting became one of the most discussed early UFO reports because its witnesses were not casual observers but trained airline professionals: United Air Lines Captain Emil J. Smith, First Officer Ralph Stevens, and stewardess Marty Morrow. Their testimony did not prove what the objects were, but it changed the evidential character of the 1947 “flying saucer” reports by adding multiple aviation witnesses who attempted to identify the objects using normal flight experience rather than immediately assuming something extraordinary.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.project1947.com/fig/ual105.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: project1947.com">[Project 1947]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">project1947.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project 1947PROJECT 1947: UAL Flight 105, July 4, 1947 - Capt E. The credibility of the Flight 105 crew rests on several factors: the pilots’ professional background, the independent confirmation provided by Morrow, the length of the observation, and the crew’s initial attempt to interpret the objects as conventional aircraft. At the same time, the case illustrates a central issue in UFO investigations: reliable witnesses can provide strong evidence that an unusual observation occurred, while leaving open the separate question of what was actually observed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.project1947.com/fig/ual105.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: project1947.com">[Project 1947]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">project1947.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project 1947PROJECT 1947: UAL Flight 105, July 4, 1947 - Capt E. J. Smith...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 4, 1947</span></span></span>

## Why the Flight 105 witnesses stood out

### Captain Smith and Ralph Stevens observations

The two pilots formed the core of the report. Flight 105 departed Boise, Idaho, on the evening of 4 July 1947, with Captain Emil J. Smith in command and First Officer Ralph Stevens at the controls. According to contemporary accounts, Stevens first noticed an object that he believed might be another aircraft. He responded as an aviator would in a potentially confusing airspace situation: he flashed the DC-3’s landing lights as a signal. Only after observing the objects more carefully did the pilots conclude that they could not identify conventional aircraft features such as wings or tails.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://project1947.com/fig/1947d.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: project1947.com">[Project 1947]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">project1947.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project 1947PROJECT 1947 - UFO REPORTS: 1947...</span></span></span>

This detail became important for later assessments of credibility. The pilots were not initially looking for confirmation of a UFO claim; their first interpretation was ordinary aviation traffic. Supporters of the case have argued that this sequence reduced the likelihood that they simply mislabelled an unfamiliar sight because of pre-existing expectations. Smith, in particular, had publicly expressed scepticism about earlier “flying saucer” reports before his own sighting, reportedly joking that he would believe in such objects when he saw them himself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.project1947.com/fig/ual105.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: project1947.com">[Project 1947]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">project1947.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project 1947PROJECT 1947: UAL Flight 105, July 4, 1947 - Capt E. J. Smith...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 4, 1947</span></span></span>

The pilots reported seeing several disc-like objects, including a larger object among a formation. They described watching the objects for several minutes and reported changes in formation before the objects disappeared. Later accounts varied in exact details, including numbers and movements, but the central testimony remained consistent: the crew saw unusual objects in the sky during the flight and could not match them to known aircraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.project1947.com/fig/ual105.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: project1947.com">[Project 1947]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">project1947.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project 1947PROJECT 1947: UAL Flight 105, July 4, 1947 - Capt E. J. Smith...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 4, 1947</span></span></span>

The significance of Smith and Stevens was therefore not simply that they were experienced observers, but that their professional training shaped how they approached the event. They tried to classify the objects within normal aviation categories before treating them as unidentified. This made the case more influential among UFO researchers than many contemporary reports from single, untrained witnesses.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.project1947.com/fig/ual105.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: project1947.com">[Project 1947]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">project1947.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project 1947PROJECT 1947: UAL Flight 105, July 4, 1947 - Capt E. J. Smith...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 4, 1947</span></span></span>

## Marty Morrow’s role as an independent witness

The presence of stewardess Marty Morrow strengthened the case because she provided a third observation from outside the pilots’ immediate decision-making process. After Smith and Stevens noticed the objects, they called Morrow to the cockpit so she could view them herself. Contemporary reports state that she confirmed seeing the objects, adding a separate witness who was not responsible for interpreting the flight situation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://project1947.com/fig/1947d.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: project1947.com">[Project 1947]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">project1947.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project 1947PROJECT 1947 - UFO REPORTS: 1947...</span></span></span>

Her testimony mattered because multi-witness cases are often considered stronger than single-observer reports when the witnesses make their observations independently. In Flight 105’s case, the witnesses shared the same environment and viewing conditions, so their accounts were not completely independent. However, Morrow’s involvement meant the report was not based solely on the perceptions of the two pilots who were already discussing what they were seeing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.project1947.com/fig/ual105.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: project1947.com">[Project 1947]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">project1947.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project 1947PROJECT 1947: UAL Flight 105, July 4, 1947 - Capt E. J. Smith...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 4, 1947</span></span></span>

The case also contained an important limitation: although the crew saw the objects, no passenger testimony became part of the main record. The available accounts emphasise the cockpit view, where the pilots had the clearest line of sight, rather than a broad cabin-wide observation. The lack of passenger confirmation did not invalidate the crew’s report, but it meant that the number of direct witnesses remained limited.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_105_UFO_sighting" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Flight 105 UFO sighting</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Flight 105 UFO sighting</span></span></span>

## What credibility can and cannot establish

### Strengths of the testimony

The strongest argument for the Flight 105 witnesses is the combination of professional experience and contemporaneous reporting. The witnesses were aviation personnel familiar with aircraft identification, weather conditions, and normal flight operations. The event was also reported quickly, with Smith communicating the sighting to aviation personnel after the flight, which reduced concerns that the account was created only after later UFO publicity developed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.project1947.com/fig/ual105.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: project1947.com">[Project 1947]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">project1947.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project 1947PROJECT 1947: UAL Flight 105, July 4, 1947 - Capt E. J. Smith...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 4, 1947</span></span></span>

UFO researcher James E. McDonald later highlighted the case as historically important because it involved experienced observers, a prolonged observation period, and multiple witnesses. In testimony prepared for a 1968 US congressional symposium, McDonald described Flight 105 as a significant unidentified case because of these factors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.project1947.com/fig/ual105.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: project1947.com">[Project 1947]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">project1947.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project 1947PROJECT 1947: UAL Flight 105, July 4, 1947 - Capt E. J. Smith...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 4, 1947</span></span></span>

The crew’s behaviour during the sighting also contributed to its reputation. They attempted radio communication and sought possible confirmation rather than simply reporting an extraordinary event without investigation. This pattern of cautious observation is one reason the case remained prominent in later discussions of witness reliability.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.project1947.com/fig/ual105.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: project1947.com">[Project 1947]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">project1947.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project 1947PROJECT 1947: UAL Flight 105, July 4, 1947 - Capt E. ### Limits of eyewitness evidence

Despite these strengths, experienced witnesses are not immune to misidentification. Aviation professionals are highly trained in recognising aircraft, but unusual observations can still be affected by distance estimates, lighting conditions, perspective, atmospheric effects, and assumptions about speed or movement. A witness can be entirely honest and accurately describe what they perceived while still being mistaken about the object’s identity.

This distinction became central to official UFO investigations. Later Air Force studies examined large numbers of sightings and concluded that many reports could be explained by ordinary aircraft, balloons, astronomical objects, atmospheric phenomena, or misperception, while some remained unidentified because information was insufficient.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.af.mil/About-Us/Fact-Sheets/Display/Article/104590/unidentified-flying-objects-and-air-force-project-blue-book/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: af.mil">[U.S. Air Force]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on af.mil.</span></span></span> Flight 105’s lasting status came from the fact that the crew’s observations were credible as observations, even though credibility alone could not determine the physical explanation.

## Why Flight 105 remained influential

The importance of the Flight 105 testimony lies in the gap between observation and explanation. The case provided one of the earliest examples in the modern UFO era where trained aviation personnel publicly reported an unexplained aerial event. That gave the 1947 wave of sightings a new level of public attention because the witnesses appeared qualified to judge ordinary aircraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.project1947.com/fig/ual105.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: project1947.com">[Project 1947]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">project1947.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project 1947PROJECT 1947: UAL Flight 105, July 4, 1947 - Capt E. For supporters of the case, the crew’s professionalism remains the strongest reason to take the report seriously. For sceptical analysts, the same evidence demonstrates a different lesson: credible witnesses increase confidence that something unusual was seen, but they do not by themselves establish that the object represented unknown technology or an extraordinary origin.

Flight 105 therefore remains valuable as a study in witness credibility itself. It shows both the strength of trained observers in documenting unusual events and the limits of human perception when the available evidence does not include physical traces, recordings, or independent measurements.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.project1947.com/fig/ual105.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: project1947.com">[Project 1947]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">project1947.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project 1947PROJECT 1947: UAL Flight 105, July 4, 1947 - Capt E.
