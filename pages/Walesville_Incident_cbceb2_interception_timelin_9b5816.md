## Introduction

The Griffiss interception sequence is the part of the [Walesville Incident]({{ 'walesville-incident-1954/' | relative_url }}) that connects an unusual aerial report with the fatal F-94C Starfire crash of 2 July 1954. The key event was not simply a fighter accident: a Griffiss Air Force Base interceptor was diverted from training into an air-defence mission after controllers received reports of unidentified aircraft in the area. The pilot successfully identified one target as an Air Force C-47 transport, but a second reported target remained the focus of later debate because the aircraft crashed shortly after being vectored towards it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/walesville_condon.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[NICAP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">walesville_condonJuly 2, 1954...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 2, 1954</span></span></span>

The surviving record shows a genuine military interception response, but it does not provide clear evidence that the unknown target caused the crash. The strongest documented timeline establishes three linked facts: aerial reports reached Griffiss, an F-94C was redirected to investigate, and the interceptor was lost after an onboard emergency during the mission. The unresolved point is whether the second aerial report represented an unusual object, an unidentified conventional aircraft, or a reporting and identification problem during a Cold War air-defence operation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/walesville_condon.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[NICAP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">walesville_condonJuly 2, 1954...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 2, 1954</span></span></span>

## Reports from Griffiss Air Force Base

The first reports associated with the incident came from the Griffiss area near Rome, New York, where the US Air Force maintained interceptor operations during the early Cold War. According to the later Air Force account reproduced in the Condon Report, personnel at the Air Force facility in Rome received reports on 1 July 1954 of an object described as having a balloon-like appearance. The officer responsible reportedly believed it was probably a partially deflated balloon but indicated it would be investigated if it remained visible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/walesville_condon.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[NICAP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">walesville_condonJuly 2, 1954...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 2, 1954</span></span></span>

This initial report became important because it created the background for the following day’s air-defence activity. Later UFO researchers argued that the sequence showed a sustained period of concern around an unidentified object near restricted airspace. Other investigators have viewed the balloon report as a normal example of Cold War-era misidentification, where weather balloons, aircraft, and other airborne objects could trigger military interest without indicating an extraordinary event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/540702walesville_ridge.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[NICAP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFO Report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO Report...</span></span></span>

The available evidence does not show a continuous visual track from the first report to the later interception. Instead, the case consists of separate reports and responses occurring in the same area and time window. This distinction matters because later retellings often merge the balloon-like sighting, the unidentified aircraft report, and the crash into a single encounter. The documentary record is more cautious: it shows an interception mission, but not a confirmed identity for every reported object involved.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/walesville_condon.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[NICAP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">walesville_condonJuly 2, 1954...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 2, 1954</span></span></span>

## The F-94 diversion and target identification

At 11:05 local time on 2 July 1954, F-94C Starfire aircraft 51-13559 departed Griffiss Air Force Base on what was initially described as a routine training mission. Shortly after take-off, ground-controlled interception (GCI) personnel redirected the aircraft to an active air-defence task. The crew was instructed to intercept an unidentified aircraft at approximately 10,000 feet.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/walesville_condon.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[NICAP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">walesville_condonJuly 2, 1954...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 2, 1954</span></span></span>

The F-94 was designed specifically for this type of mission. It was an all-weather interceptor equipped with radar and intended to locate and engage aircraft approaching defended airspace. In the Walesville sequence, however, the first target did not remain unidentified for long. The pilot visually identified it as a USAF C-47 transport aircraft, including its tail number, resolving that part of the intercept.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/walesville_condon.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[NICAP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">walesville_condonJuly 2, 1954...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 2, 1954</span></span></span>

Controllers then directed the crew towards another reported aircraft. The second target is the central uncertainty in the timeline. The official account states that the pilot was instructed to investigate another unidentified aircraft at low altitude, apparently descending towards Griffiss. Later researchers have disagreed over how to interpret this detail: some argue it demonstrates that the interceptor was responding to a genuine unknown object, while others argue that an unidentified radar or visual contact during an active defence mission does not necessarily indicate an extraordinary craft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/540702walesville_ridge.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[NICAP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFO Report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO Report...</span></span></span>

A major point of confusion is that the word “unidentified” had a practical military meaning. In air-defence operations, it often meant only that controllers had not yet matched a radar or visual contact with a known flight plan. It did not automatically mean the object displayed impossible performance or technology. The Walesville record therefore documents an unresolved identification problem rather than a confirmed unknown vehicle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/walesville_condon.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[NICAP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">walesville_condonJuly 2, 1954...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 2, 1954</span></span></span>

## The emergency that ended the interception

The interception ended before the F-94 crew could complete identification of the second target. During descent, the pilot reported an abrupt rise in cockpit temperature and observed the engine fire warning light. After reducing power and finding the warning persisted, the pilot shut down the engine. Both crew members — pilot Lieutenant William E. Atkins and radar observer Lieutenant Henry F. Coudon — ejected successfully.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/walesville_condon.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[NICAP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">walesville_condonJuly 2, 1954...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 2, 1954</span></span></span>

The unmanned aircraft continued flying and crashed at the Walesville intersection, destroying the aircraft and killing four civilians on the ground. Aviation accident records identify the aircraft as F-94C Starfire 51-13559, operated by the US Air Force from Griffiss Air Force Base.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://aviation-safety.net/wikibase/180615" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aviation-safety.net">[Aviation Safety Network]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aviation-safety.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aviation Safety NetworkAccident Lockheed F-94C Starfire 51-13559, Friday 2 July 1954...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 1954</span></span></span>

The crash sequence itself created the lasting association between the interception report and the Walesville Incident. The unusual timing — an unidentified aerial report followed by a fighter emergency during an interception — encouraged later interpretations that the two events were physically connected. However, the official accident narrative treated the loss as an aircraft malfunction during an air-defence mission rather than as the result of contact with an unknown object.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/walesville_condon.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[NICAP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">walesville_condonJuly 2, 1954...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 2, 1954</span></span></span>

## What the timeline proves and leaves uncertain

The Griffiss timeline establishes several points with relatively strong documentary support:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Aerial reports reached Air Force personnel in the Griffiss area before the crash.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/walesville_condon.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[NICAP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">walesville_condonJuly 2, 1954...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 2, 1954</span></span></span>

What remains uncertain is the nature of the second target. UFO researchers have treated this gap as the central mystery of the case, arguing that an unresolved target during a military interception deserves further attention. Skeptical analyses emphasise that an unidentified contact is not the same as evidence of an unusual craft and that the crash investigation did not establish a connection between the target report and the aircraft failure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/540702walesville_ridge.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[NICAP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFO Report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO Report...</span></span></span>

The most balanced reading of the Griffiss interception timeline is therefore narrower than many later retellings suggest. The incident demonstrates how Cold War air-defence procedures could rapidly turn uncertain aerial reports into fighter interceptions, and how a subsequent accident could become linked to the original report. The evidence confirms the interception and the uncertainty surrounding the target; it does not independently confirm that the unknown report represented a non-conventional object or caused the F-94’s failure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/walesville_condon.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[NICAP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">walesville_condonJuly 2, 1954...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 2, 1954</span></span></span>
