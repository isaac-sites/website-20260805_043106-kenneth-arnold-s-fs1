## Introduction

Kenneth Arnold did not set out to invent the phrase “flying saucer”. Instead, a combination of his own vivid comparisons, compressed newspaper reporting and eye-catching headlines transformed a detailed pilot's account into the defining image of modern UFO culture. Within days of his 24 June 1947 sighting near Mount Rainier, reports across the United States were referring to mysterious “flying saucers”, even though Arnold repeatedly maintained that his most famous comparison described the objects' motion rather than their exact shape. The episode is an important case study in how eyewitness testimony can be reframed by the media, with lasting consequences for public imagination and later reports.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://airandspace.si.edu/stories/editorial/1947-year-flying-saucer" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airandspace.si.edu">[National Air and Space Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airandspace.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1947 year flying saucer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Air and Space Museum1947: Year of the Flying Saucer &#124; National Air and Space MuseumJune 24, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 24, 2022</span></span></span>

## Arnold's Original Comparisons: Shape and Motion Were Not the Same Thing

One of the most persistent myths surrounding Kenneth Arnold's sighting is that he simply reported seeing saucer-shaped aircraft. His actual descriptions were more nuanced.

Arnold consistently described the objects as bright, thin and apparently tailless, but his comparisons varied depending on whether he was discussing their appearance or their movement. During early interviews he likened them to pie plates or flat pans in some respects, while later sketches submitted to Army Air Forces intelligence showed a shape with a rounded front and a tapered or crescent-like rear rather than a perfect disc. The famous sketch resembles neither the classic circular flying saucer nor a conventional aircraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://airandspace.si.edu/stories/editorial/1947-year-flying-saucer" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airandspace.si.edu">[National Air and Space Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airandspace.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1947 year flying saucer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Air and Space Museum1947: Year of the Flying Saucer &#124; National Air and Space MuseumJune 24, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 24, 2022</span></span></span>

His most memorable description concerned how they flew. Arnold explained that they moved:

> "Like a saucer if you skip it across the water."

The analogy referred to an oscillating, skipping motion as the objects appeared to weave along the mountain horizon. Arnold later argued that many journalists interpreted this movement comparison as a description of the objects' shape. He repeatedly complained that newspapers had misunderstood or oversimplified what he had said.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.history.com/articles/kenneth-arnold" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: history.com">[Sky HISTORY TV channel]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">history.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Sky HISTORY TV channel Kenneth Arnold</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sky HISTORY TV channelKenneth Arnold - UFO, 1947 &amp; Flying Saucer &#124; HISTORY...</span></span></span>

This distinction matters because it shows that the iconic image of a perfectly circular "flying saucer" did not originate directly from Arnold's testimony but from the way his words were interpreted and retold.

## How the *East Oregonian* Helped Create a New Label

After landing, Arnold discussed the incident with other pilots before arriving in Pendleton, Oregon. There he spoke with reporters from the *East Oregonian*, particularly Bill Bequette, whose coverage became the foundation for national reporting.

The first local stories described mysterious, bright objects travelling at extraordinary speed and used phrases such as "saucer-like" to summarise Arnold's description. Although these articles conveyed much of Arnold's account accurately, the inevitable compression of a complicated eyewitness narrative into newspaper prose encouraged readers to associate "saucer" primarily with appearance rather than movement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.project1947.com/fig/1947c.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: project1947.com">[project1947.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">project1947.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PROJECT 1947 - UFO REPORTS: 1947...</span></span></span>

Once wire services such as the Associated Press and United Press distributed the story nationally, the wording became even shorter and more standardised. Headlines proved especially influential because editors needed concise, memorable phrases that would attract attention.

Examples included descriptions such as:

* "Saucer-like objects"<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.thelivingmoon.com/49ufo_files/03files2/1947_Kenneth_Arnold.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thelivingmoon.com">[thelivingmoon.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thelivingmoon.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kenneth Arnold</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kenneth Arnold</span></span></span> * "Flying discs" * "Flying saucers"(#endnote-17 "Endnote 17")<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ijQl3I-6oiE" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: youtube.com">[youtube.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">youtube.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Origin of Flying Saucers</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The UFO Deluge of July 4th, 1947 &#124; Part 1: Omens (Before the Storm)...</span></span></span>

## Why "Flying Saucer" Spread So Quickly

The phrase succeeded because it was simple, visual and easy to remember.

Several factors reinforced its rapid adoption:

* **A striking metaphor.** Nearly everyone understood the image of a saucer skipping across water, making Arnold's comparison highly memorable.
* **Wire-service distribution.** National news agencies circulated the story to newspapers throughout the United States within hours, giving editors a shared vocabulary.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://airandspace.si.edu/stories/editorial/1947-year-flying-saucer" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airandspace.si.edu">[National Air and Space Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airandspace.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1947 year flying saucer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Air and Space Museum1947: Year of the Flying Saucer &#124; National Air and Space MuseumJune 24, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 24, 2022</span></span></span>
* **Headline economy.** "Flying saucer" was shorter and more dramatic than a lengthy explanation about bright, fast-moving, crescent-shaped objects.
* **Public fascination with aviation.** In 1947 Americans closely followed advances in military aircraft and expected dramatic technological breakthroughs. Reports of objects apparently travelling faster than known aircraft naturally attracted attention.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://airandspace.si.edu/stories/editorial/1947-year-flying-saucer" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airandspace.si.edu">[National Air and Space Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airandspace.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1947 year flying saucer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Air and Space Museum1947: Year of the Flying Saucer &#124; National Air and Space MuseumJune 24, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 24, 2022</span></span></span>

By 27 June—only three days after [the sighting]({{ 'the-sighting/' | relative_url }})—the expression "flying saucer" had become a recognised news term appearing in newspapers across the country.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wordorigins.org/big-list-entries/flying-saucer" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wordorigins.org">[Wordorigins.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wordorigins.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">flying saucer / UFO — Wordorigins.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">flying saucer / UFO — Wordorigins.org</span></span></span>

## How the New Label Changed What People Expected to See

Once "flying saucer" entered everyday language, it began shaping later eyewitness reports.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://airandspace.si.edu/stories/editorial/1947-year-flying-saucer" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airandspace.si.edu">[airandspace.si.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airandspace.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1947 year flying saucer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Air and Space Museum1947: Year of the Flying Saucer &#124; National Air and Space MuseumJune 24, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 24, 2022</span></span></span>

Rather than describing unfamiliar aerial objects in their own words, many witnesses now used the already familiar label. As a result, reports increasingly mentioned circular or disc-shaped craft, even though Arnold's own description had been considerably more complex.

Historians of the phenomenon have noted that the label effectively established a visual template. The phrase encouraged people to imagine a specific object before they had even read the details of individual sightings. This is an example of how language can influence perception: a memorable description becomes a mental model against which later observations are interpreted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://airandspace.si.edu/stories/editorial/1947-year-flying-saucer" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airandspace.si.edu">[si.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airandspace.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1947 year flying saucer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Air and Space Museum1947: Year of the Flying Saucer &#124; National Air and Space MuseumJune 24, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 24, 2022</span></span></span>

This process also influenced popular culture. Magazine illustrations, newspaper cartoons and later science-fiction films overwhelmingly depicted smooth, circular discs, reinforcing the image that newspaper headlines had popularised.

## Arnold's Attempts to Correct the Record

Arnold spent years explaining that the press had simplified his remarks.

In later interviews he stressed that he had not intended to claim that every object resembled a dinner plate. He maintained that his comparison referred primarily to their erratic, skipping movement. He also expressed frustration over what he considered repeated misquotation and oversimplification in newspaper coverage, saying that inaccurate reporting had followed him throughout his life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://airandspace.si.edu/stories/editorial/1947-year-flying-saucer" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airandspace.si.edu">[National Air and Space Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airandspace.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1947 year flying saucer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Air and Space Museum1947: Year of the Flying Saucer &#124; National Air and Space MuseumJune 24, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 24, 2022</span></span></span>

At the same time, historians caution against treating the "misquote" explanation as entirely straightforward. Some of Arnold's earliest interviews also included comparisons to pie plates, flat pans and similar objects, meaning that both shape and motion analogies appeared in the contemporary record. The historical [evidence]({{ 'evidence/' | relative_url }}) therefore suggests not a complete fabrication by journalists, but a gradual narrowing of a richer description into one dominant image.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wordorigins.org/big-list-entries/flying-saucer" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wordorigins.org">[wordorigins.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wordorigins.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">flying saucer / UFO — Wordorigins.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">flying saucer / UFO — Wordorigins.org</span></span></span>

## A Lasting Legacy of One Phrase


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

The transformation of Arnold's account into "flying saucers" illustrates how media framing can shape the cultural meaning of an event as much as the event itself.

</div>

Whether Arnold witnessed an unusual atmospheric phenomenon, distant aircraft, mirages or something genuinely unidentified remains a separate question. What is historically well documented is the mechanism by which his testimony evolved into a powerful cultural symbol. A pilot's attempt to explain an unfamiliar flight pattern became a headline, the headline became a catchphrase, and the catchphrase became the defining image of the early UFO era. By the time the first nationwide wave of similar reports appeared in the weeks following Arnold's sighting, "flying saucer" had already become the language through which many Americans interpreted what they thought they saw in the sky.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://airandspace.si.edu/stories/editorial/1947-year-flying-saucer" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airandspace.si.edu">[si.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airandspace.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1947 year flying saucer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Air and Space Museum1947: Year of the Flying Saucer &#124; National Air and Space MuseumJune 24, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 24, 2022</span></span></span>
