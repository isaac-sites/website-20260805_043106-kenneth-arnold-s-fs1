## Charles B. Moore’s scientific background

Moore’s background is the main reason the sighting attracted official attention. At the time of the incident, he was associated with General Mills Aeronautical Research and had extensive experience with high-altitude balloon work. Earlier in his career he had been involved in advanced balloon research for government programmes, giving him practical knowledge of atmospheric conditions, balloon behaviour, and observational techniques.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/htm/arrey49.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOs at close sight: Arrey, New Mexico, April 24, 1949...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 24, 1949</span></span></span>

This expertise mattered because the observation occurred during a normal scientific operation rather than during a search for unusual phenomena. Moore and four Navy personnel were conducting a pilot-balloon measurement run near Arrey, New Mexico, connected with upper-atmosphere research. A theodolite — an optical instrument used to measure angles and track moving objects — was already being used as part of the work. When Moore noticed an object in the sky, he initially believed it might be the balloon being tracked. Only after checking the instrument alignment did he conclude that the object was something else.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/htm/arrey49.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOs at close sight: Arrey, New Mexico, April 24, 1949...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 24, 1949</span></span></span>

A 1949 Special Devices Center communication forwarding Moore’s report to naval authorities specifically highlighted his qualifications. It described him as a trained engineer and experienced observer, noting his previous balloon research experience and treating his statement as significant because it included detailed theodolite tracking information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/htm/arrey49.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOs at close sight: Arrey, New Mexico, April 24, 1949...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 24, 1949</span></span></span>

That professional background does not remove the normal limitations of human observation, but it changes the nature of the evidence. Moore was not simply describing an impression such as “a strange object flew overhead”; he was attempting to record an event using the methods available to him as a technical observer.

## The Navy observers and their accounts

Moore was not alone in making the report. Four Navy personnel working with him — Chief Akers and crew members Davidson, Fitzsimmons, and Moorman — were present during the balloon observation. Their accounts supported the basic claim that an unusual object was seen while the team was carrying out its assigned work.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/htm/arrey49.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOs at close sight: Arrey, New Mexico, April 24, 1949...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 24, 1949</span></span></span>

The strongest aspect of this group testimony is the shared context. The witnesses were already engaged in a structured activity, knew one another professionally, and were observing the same part of the sky. The observation was not a later reconstruction based on rumours or a widely circulated story. Moore’s description developed from an event that occurred while the team was using instruments and recording atmospheric data.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/htm/arrey49.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOs at close sight: Arrey, New Mexico, April 24, 1949...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 24, 1949</span></span></span>

The group evidence also has limits. Moore was the person with the greatest technical role in interpreting the sighting, particularly the object’s apparent movement through the theodolite. The other witnesses helped confirm that an object was present, but they did not independently produce the same level of measurement and analysis. As a result, the case’s credibility rests heavily on Moore’s professional judgement rather than on several independent technical measurements.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/Good_Cases/490424arrey_dir.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[Nicap]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFO Report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO Report...</span></span></span>

This distinction is important. Multiple witnesses can increase confidence that something was observed, but they do not automatically confirm every conclusion drawn from that observation. The presence of several observers supports the reality of the event; it does not by itself establish the object’s speed, altitude, or origin.

## Why the theodolite observation strengthened the case

The use of the theodolite is the feature that most separates the Moore sighting from many other early UFO reports. Moore reported tracking a white elliptical object through a 25-power telescope and estimating its angular movement across the sky. The team recorded details such as direction changes and apparent elevation changes during the roughly one-minute observation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/htm/arrey49.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOs at close sight: Arrey, New Mexico, April 24, 1949...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 24, 1949</span></span></span>

For investigators, this created a more substantial record than an ordinary visual sighting. The Special Devices Center considered the report noteworthy because the changes in direction and elevation appeared potentially relevant to questions of controlled flight.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/htm/arrey49.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOs at close sight: Arrey, New Mexico, April 24, 1949...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 24, 1949</span></span></span>

However, the instrument did not directly measure all the quantities later associated with the case. Theodolite observations can accurately record angular movement, but determining true speed and distance requires knowing how far away an object is. In the Moore case, estimates of altitude and velocity depended on assumptions about the object’s range. Later calculations using assumed distances produced very high speeds, but those figures were not direct measurements of the object’s actual motion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/Good_Cases/490424arrey_dir.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[Nicap]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFO Report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO Report...</span></span></span>

This is where credibility and interpretation separate. Moore’s skill with the instrument supports confidence that he accurately recorded what he saw through the telescope. It does not necessarily prove that the object behaved in the way later interpretations suggested.

## Strengths and limits of eyewitness evidence

The Moore case illustrates both the value and the difficulty of expert eyewitness testimony. A trained observer can recognise ordinary [explanations]({{ 'explanations-e0cbd7/' | relative_url }}), understand equipment limitations, and provide more detailed information than an untrained witness. Moore’s professional experience makes his account more significant than a simple report of an unexplained light in the sky.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/htm/arrey49.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOs at close sight: Arrey, New Mexico, April 24, 1949...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 24, 1949</span></span></span>

At the same time, expertise does not eliminate uncertainty. Even experienced observers can misjudge distance, scale, and speed when viewing an unfamiliar object without independent reference points. Atmospheric conditions, optical effects, and incomplete information can influence interpretation. These issues are especially important in aerial observations, where a small object at an unknown distance can appear to move dramatically.

## Why Moore’s testimony still matters

Moore’s credibility remains the defining feature of the 1949 sighting because it places the case in a different category from many contemporary reports. The lasting question is not whether a trained scientist saw something unusual — the available records strongly support that he did. The continuing debate concerns what that observation represents.

For supporters of the case’s importance, Moore demonstrates that unexplained aerial observations were sometimes reported by technically capable people using serious methods. For sceptical interpretations, the case demonstrates a different lesson: even careful observers working with instruments can produce data that remain ambiguous without independent confirmation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/Good_Cases/490424arrey_dir.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[Nicap]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFO Report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO Report...</span></span></span>

The enduring value of Moore’s testimony lies in that middle ground. It is a historically important example of a carefully documented observation, but its credibility as an observation should not be confused with proof of any particular explanation for the object seen over New Mexico in 1949.
