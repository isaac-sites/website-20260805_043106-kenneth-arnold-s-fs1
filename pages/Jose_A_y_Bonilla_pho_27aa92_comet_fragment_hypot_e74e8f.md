## Introduction

The comet fragment hypothesis is the most ambitious modern reinterpretation of the José A. y Bonilla photograph of 1883. It proposes that the dark shapes recorded at the Zacatecas Observatory were not unknown aircraft or ordinary nearby objects, but pieces of a comet that had broken apart and passed extremely close to Earth. The idea emerged in 2011 from researchers at the National Autonomous University of Mexico, who used Bonilla’s recorded timings, apparent motion, and descriptions of the objects to model a possible fragmented comet encounter.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1110.2798" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Interpretation of the observations made in 1883 in Zacatecas (Mexico): A fragmented Comet that nearly hits the EarthOctober 12, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 12, 2011</span></span></span>

The proposal changed the way [the photographs]({{ 'the-photographs/' | relative_url }}) were discussed because it offered a natural astronomical explanation that could account for several unusual features of the observation: the large number of objects, their apparent movement across the Sun, their reported fuzzy appearance, and the dark trails described by Bonilla. However, the comet interpretation remains a debated reconstruction rather than an established identification. The available evidence supports the possibility of a fragmented comet, but it does not conclusively rule out other explanations such as objects close to the telescope.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonilla_observation" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Bonilla observation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bonilla observation</span></span></span>

## The 2011 comet fragment proposal

The modern comet explanation was developed in the 2011 paper *Interpretation of the observations made in 1883 in Zacatecas (Mexico): A fragmented Comet that nearly hits the Earth*. Authors Héctor Javier Durand Manterola, María de la Paz Ramos Lara, and Guadalupe Cordero revisited Bonilla’s original observations with modern astronomical calculations. Their central argument was that a fragmented comet passing close to Earth could produce the kind of apparent solar transits recorded at Zacatecas.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1110.2798" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Interpretation of the observations made in 1883 in Zacatecas (Mexico): A fragmented Comet that nearly hits the EarthOctober 12, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 12, 2011</span></span></span>

The researchers focused on several details from Bonilla’s report. He observed hundreds of objects crossing the Sun on 12 and 13 August 1883 and recorded many of them photographically. The objects were described as indistinct or cloud-like, and some were reported to have trails. A swarm of comet fragments could naturally produce multiple dark silhouettes rather than a single solid object, especially if the fragments were surrounded by dust and gas released during breakup.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonilla_observation" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Bonilla observation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bonilla observation</span></span></span>

The proposal also attempted to explain why the event was apparently limited to Bonilla’s location rather than being widely reported. The researchers argued that the geometry of a very close pass, combined with parallax — the apparent shift of an object when viewed from different locations — could mean that only observers in a narrow region would have seen the fragments crossing the Sun in the same way.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonilla_observation" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Bonilla observation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bonilla observation</span></span></span>

## Astronomical clues and calculations

The strongest part of the comet hypothesis comes from its attempt to convert Bonilla’s visual record into physical estimates. By analysing the apparent size and movement of the objects across the solar disc, the 2011 study estimated that the fragments may have passed between roughly 538 kilometres and 8,062 kilometres above Earth’s surface. The estimated fragment widths ranged from tens to hundreds of metres, with the researchers proposing that the original comet could have had a mass comparable in scale to a small fraction of, or several times, the mass of Halley’s Comet depending on the assumptions used.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1110.2798" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Interpretation of the observations made in 1883 in Zacatecas (Mexico): A fragmented Comet that nearly hits the EarthOctober 12, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 12, 2011</span></span></span>

The calculations were based on interpreting Bonilla’s observations as a close atmospheric-scale encounter rather than a distant astronomical transit. If the fragments had really passed at such distances, the event would have been an unusually close cometary flyby. Modern astronomy has documented that comet nuclei can break apart into multiple pieces, producing fragment clouds and dust structures, making the general mechanism physically plausible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1602.07569" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv The catastrophic fragmentation of Comet 332P (Ikeya-Murakami</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The catastrophic fragmentation of Comet 332P (Ikeya-Murakami)February 24, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 24, 2016</span></span></span>

One reason the hypothesis attracted attention was the dramatic implication: a fragmented comet passing close to Earth would represent a major planetary hazard scenario. The 2011 authors estimated that a direct collision with many large fragments could have produced consequences comparable in scale to multiple Tunguska-like impacts. Those estimates, however, depend heavily on the assumed fragment sizes, composition, and trajectory, all of which are reconstructed rather than directly measured.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://phys.org/news/2011-10-mexican-astronomers-bonilla-sighting-comet.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: phys.org">[Phys.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">phys.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on phys.org.</span></span></span>

The comet interpretation also gives a different meaning to the photographs themselves. Instead of being images of unexplained “objects” in the modern UFO sense, they would represent a rare astronomical event captured with early photographic technology: the passage of a fragmenting natural body through Earth’s neighbourhood. The photographs would therefore remain historically important while having a conventional astronomical explanation.

## Why the theory remains debated

The main challenge for the comet explanation is that it requires several uncertain assumptions to align. Bonilla’s photographs and notes provide evidence that something crossed the telescope’s view, but they do not provide direct measurements of distance, size, speed, or composition. The comet model must infer those values from limited 19th-century observations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonilla_observation" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Bonilla observation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bonilla observation</span></span></span>

Sceptics have pointed out that the original astronomical discussion of the event did not identify a comet. When Bonilla’s report appeared in *L’Astronomie* in 1886, the explanation considered more likely at the time was that nearby objects such as birds or insects had passed close to the telescope and appeared as silhouettes against the bright Sun. This interpretation explains why the event was not widely observed by other astronomers, although it also has difficulties explaining every detail of Bonilla’s description.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonilla_observation" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Bonilla observation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bonilla observation</span></span></span>
