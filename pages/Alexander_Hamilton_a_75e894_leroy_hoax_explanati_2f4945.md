## Introduction

The Le Roy airship story is best understood today as a famous example of a nineteenth-century tall tale that was later absorbed into UFO history. In April 1897, Kansas farmer [Alexander Hamilton]({{ 'alexander-hamilton-airship-1897/' | relative_url }}) reported that a huge airship had descended over his property and carried away a heifer. The story appeared convincing because it included named witnesses, a sworn affidavit supporting Hamilton’s character, and a vivid description of an airborne craft. Later investigations, however, connected the incident to local storytelling traditions and concluded that the episode was probably an intentional hoax rather than evidence of an unknown aircraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.howstuffworks.com/space/aliens-ufos/cow-abduction.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.howstuffworks.com">[HowStuffWorks Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.howstuffworks.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cow abduction</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HowStuffWorks ScienceThe 1897 Cow Abduction Hoax &#124; HowStuffWorks...</span></span></span>

The importance of the Le Roy case is not only the question of whether Hamilton invented the event, but how a community joke became one of the most frequently discussed incidents in early UFO literature. The case shows how newspaper culture, local folklore, and later mystery investigations could transform a regional story into a lasting legend.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historynet.com/people-and-planes/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historynet.com">[HistoryNet]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historynet.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">people and planes</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A &#x27;balloon traveling end on…and with what appeared to be wings&#x27;: The Great Airship Mystery of the 1800sMarch 31, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 31, 2016</span></span></span>

## The later investigation by UFO researchers

When UFO researchers revisited the Alexander Hamilton airship account decades after 1897, they initially treated it as one of the strongest examples from the wider “mystery airship” wave of 1896–1897. The original story had many features that attracted investigators: multiple apparent witnesses, a contemporary newspaper report, and a claim of physical consequences involving a missing animal.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.howstuffworks.com/space/aliens-ufos/cow-abduction.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.howstuffworks.com">[HowStuffWorks Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.howstuffworks.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cow abduction</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HowStuffWorks ScienceThe 1897 Cow Abduction Hoax &#124; HowStuffWorks...</span></span></span>

The reassessment changed during the 1970s, particularly through the work of UFO researcher Jerome Clark. Clark investigated the background of the Le Roy story and reported evidence that Hamilton’s account had originated as a deliberate piece of local humour. Later summaries of the investigation describe interviews and historical recollections indicating that the airship-and-cow story was created as an exaggerated tale rather than a factual report.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mystery_airship" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mystery airship</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mystery airship</span></span></span>

A key part of the later explanation involved testimony from people connected with the Le Roy area who remembered Hamilton as a participant in a local “Liars’ Club” tradition. According to accounts collected by researchers, the group’s purpose was to compete in creating increasingly outrageous stories, with Hamilton’s airship tale becoming the most successful example.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.howstuffworks.com/space/aliens-ufos/cow-abduction.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.howstuffworks.com">[HowStuffWorks Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.howstuffworks.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cow abduction</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HowStuffWorks ScienceThe 1897 Cow Abduction Hoax &#124; HowStuffWorks...</span></span></span>

The investigation also changed how researchers viewed the original affidavit. In 1897, the signatures of respected local residents appeared to strengthen Hamilton’s credibility. However, the later hoax explanation suggested that the affidavit did not function as independent verification of an extraordinary event; instead, it may have been part of the joke or a reflection of local knowledge that outsiders did not share.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.howstuffworks.com/space/aliens-ufos/cow-abduction.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.howstuffworks.com">[HowStuffWorks Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.howstuffworks.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cow abduction</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HowStuffWorks ScienceThe 1897 Cow Abduction Hoax &#124; HowStuffWorks...</span></span></span>

## The Liars’ Club tradition behind the story

The Le Roy explanation fits within a broader tradition of small-town storytelling in late nineteenth-century America. Informal “liars’ clubs” and similar social groups existed as spaces where members competed through humorous exaggeration, fictional adventures, and elaborate verbal performances. Kansas itself had a documented tradition of clubs built around playful exaggeration, including the St. Ananias Club, whose name referenced a cultural association with tall tales and deliberate falsehoods.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Kansas%3A_A_Cyclopedia_of_State_History/Ananias_Club" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: en.wikisource.org">[Wikisource]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">en.wikisource.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Kansas: A Cyclopedia of State History/Ananias Club</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kansas: A Cyclopedia of State History/Ananias Club - Wikisource, the free online library...</span></span></span>

In this setting, the mystery airship craze provided an ideal subject. During 1896 and 1897, newspapers across the United States published numerous reports of strange flying machines. Some accounts reflected genuine confusion about new technology, while others were influenced by jokes, speculation, and the public appetite for unusual stories. The Le Roy tale combined all of these ingredients: a popular new mystery, a rural setting, and a storyteller seeking an unforgettable narrative.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mystery_airship" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mystery airship</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mystery airship</span></span></span>

The story’s structure also resembles a classic tall tale. It contained escalating details designed to make the account memorable: a gigantic cigar-shaped craft, unusual occupants, a cable attached to livestock, and the impossible image of a cow being carried into the sky. These details made the story entertaining and helped it spread beyond Kansas, but they also contributed to later doubts about its reliability.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historynet.com/people-and-planes/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historynet.com">[HistoryNet]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historynet.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">people and planes</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A &#x27;balloon traveling end on…and with what appeared to be wings&#x27;: The Great Airship Mystery of the 1800sMarch 31, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 31, 2016</span></span></span>

The fact that the story became widely reported does not necessarily mean every participant intended to deceive the public in a serious way. The later folklore interpretation presents the episode more as a community prank that escaped its original setting. A story created for local amusement was repeated by newspapers, encountered by distant readers without the local context, and eventually treated as a historical mystery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.spaceshipsofezekiel.com/html/misc-kansas-airship-cownapping.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: spaceshipsofezekiel.com">[Spaceships of Ezekiel]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">spaceshipsofezekiel.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Spaceships of Ezekiel The Great Kansas Cownapping Airship Hoax</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Spaceships of Ezekiel The Great Kansas Cownapping Airship Hoax</span></span></span>

## How folklore became UFO history

The Le Roy airship incident became part of UFO history because later generations encountered it without the original social context. In the 1960s and 1970s, interest in unidentified flying objects encouraged researchers to revisit earlier reports of strange aerial phenomena. The [1897 airship wave]({{ '1897-airship-wave/' | relative_url }}) appeared especially significant because it predated modern flying technology and included descriptions of advanced-looking craft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mystery_airship" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mystery airship</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mystery airship</span></span></span>

For a time, the Hamilton case was often discussed alongside other unexplained airship reports as a possible example of an early encounter with an unknown machine. However, the later hoax interpretation made Le Roy useful for a different reason: it demonstrated how a seemingly well-supported historical case could still be shaped by folklore, humour, and unreliable transmission.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mystery_airship" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mystery airship</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mystery airship</span></span></span>

The case remains important because it highlights the difference between evidence that a story was told and evidence that the event itself occurred. The newspaper article, witnesses, and affidavit prove that the claim existed in 1897. They do not, by themselves, prove that an airship removed a heifer. The later investigation added a competing explanation: that the event was a crafted story whose success depended on people outside the community taking it literally.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.howstuffworks.com/space/aliens-ufos/cow-abduction.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.howstuffworks.com">[HowStuffWorks Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.howstuffworks.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cow abduction</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HowStuffWorks ScienceThe 1897 Cow Abduction Hoax &#124; HowStuffWorks...</span></span></span>

This distinction has made Le Roy a landmark example in discussions of historical UFO reports. Rather than simply being an alleged encounter or a simple hoax, it is a case study in how rumours develop: a local joke becomes a newspaper sensation, a newspaper sensation becomes a mystery, and the mystery eventually becomes part of a larger cultural tradition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.historynet.com/people-and-planes/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historynet.com">[HistoryNet]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historynet.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">people and planes</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A &#x27;balloon traveling end on…and with what appeared to be wings&#x27;: The Great Airship Mystery of the 1800sMarch 31, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 31, 2016</span></span></span>
